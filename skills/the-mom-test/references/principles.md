# The Mom Test — Core principles

Source: *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick (v1.0). Paraphrased working summary with chapter pointers so you can verify against the book. Items marked **[skill heuristic]** are additions of this skill, not claims from the book.

## 1. The premise (Ch. 1)

- Nobody — not just your mom — should be asked "is my business a good idea?". Only the market can tell you; everything else is opinion. It is the founder's job to find the truth by asking good questions, not other people's job to hand it over.
- Customer conversations are **bad by default**. Fixing them is the interviewer's responsibility.
- A conversation is *useful* when it yields **concrete facts about the customer's life and worldview** that let you improve the business. If you leave with only a good feeling, it was not useful.
- The surprising effect of the method: you learn whether people care about your idea **by not mentioning it**. Mentioning the idea too soon (not too late) is almost always the mistake. Eventually you do have to mention it and ask for commitment.
- Division of labour: **they own the problem, you own the solution.** You may not tell them what their problem is; they may not tell you what to build. You collect information about their lives (problems, cares, constraints, goals), take your own visionary leap, then confirm it through commitment and advancement.

## 2. The three rules

1. **Talk about their life instead of your idea.**
2. **Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.**
3. **Talk less and listen more.**

Why it works: people rarely lie about specific things that already happened, regardless of your ego.

## 3. Bad data (Ch. 2)

Bad data causes false positives (you think you're right and aren't — the dangerous one) and false negatives (you think the idea is dead and it isn't). Three kinds:

| Type | What it looks like | Why it's worthless |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliments** | "I love it", "That's really cool", "Keep me in the loop", "Everybody I've talked to loves it" | Cost the speaker nothing; often politeness or a way to end the conversation. Even VCs, professional judges of the future, are wrong more than right. Exception: industry experts who built very similar businesses. |
| **Fluff** | Generic claims ("I usually / always / never"), future-tense promises ("I would / I will"), hypothetical maybes ("I might / I could"). The deadliest: "I would definitely buy that." | People are wildly optimistic about their future behavior and describe who they want to be, not who they are. |
| **Ideas** | Feature requests, suggestions, "you should also…" | You have too many ideas already; each request hides a motivation you must understand first. Feature requests are to be *understood, not obeyed*. |

Sneaky variants to watch for (Ch. 2): compliment + stalling ("Looks great, let me know when it launches"), compliment hidden at the end of useful facts, and — on your side — the symptoms back at the office: "that meeting went really well", "we're getting lots of positive feedback".

## 4. The three recovery moves (Ch. 2)

| Move | Use when | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Deflect compliments** | They compliment you/idea | Don't accept it as data. Thank, ignore, and return to facts: "How are you dealing with this at the moment?" If you slipped into pitching, apologise briefly and go back to what they were saying. |
| **Anchor fluff** | Generic / future / hypothetical statement | Pull it to a concrete past instance: "When was the last time that happened?", "Talk me through it", "How did you solve it?", "What else did you try?", "Why don't you download it now?" (puts a flaky claim to a decision). A fluffy "yes" is still a usable *anchor*. |
| **Dig beneath ideas and emotions** | A feature request, or any strong emotion (angry, embarrassed, overjoyed) | Understand the motivation: "Why do you want that?", "What would that let you do?", "How are you coping without it?", "How would that fit into your day?", "Tell me more", "What makes it so awful?", "Why haven't you been able to fix this already?", "Go on." Never write the emotion down and move to the next question. |

Worked lesson (Ch. 2): an analytics-dashboard feature request turned out to hide the real need (keeping *their* clients happy with a weekly email) — a far simpler solution existed.

## 5. What makes you generate bad data

- **Fishing for compliments**: "I'm thinking of starting a business… do you think it'll work?" You already decided and want a blessing.
- **The Pathos Problem**: once your ego is visibly on the line, people protect your feelings — even when you say "be honest, I can take it". Keep your idea and ego out of the conversation until you're ready to ask for commitment.
- **Being pitchy**: holding someone hostage until they say they like it; five minutes into a monologue they stop talking about their problems. Cut yourself off ("Whoops — I just slipped into pitch mode…").
- **Talking too much / interrupting** to correct their understanding or show you've already thought about their concern: you lose a glimpse into their mental model. *The more you're talking, the worse you're doing.*

## 6. Asking *important* questions (Ch. 3)

- A question is important when **the answer could change or disprove your business**. If an unexpected answer wouldn't change what you're doing, it wasn't important.
- Avoid trivial-but-safe questions (over-compensating after learning about bias).
- Find the scary questions with thought experiments: *"If this company failed, why would that have happened?"* and *"What would have to be true for this to be a huge success?"*
- **Be terrified of at least one question per conversation.** Founders shy away from lawyers, budgets, money — you're allowed to ask about money.
- **Love bad news.** Learning your idea is flawed after spending 10% of your budget is a win; after 100% it is a disaster. You want the truth, not a gold star.
- **Lukewarm is information.** "Umm, not sure" and "that's neat" reliably say *they don't care*. The classic error is to up your game and pitch until they say something nice. More reliable information in a "meh" than a "wow".
- **Look before you zoom.** Don't focus on one problem area before knowing it's a top-of-mind problem for them; you'll "validate" a problem you led them to. Start broad ("What are your big goals / problems right now?") and zoom only after a strong signal. If you already know the problem is a top-3 priority for the segment, you may zoom immediately. If unsure it's a must-solve problem, use "does-this-problem-matter" questions (how seriously do they take it, do they spend money/time, what tools do they use, what are they already doing to improve it, what are the 3 big things they're trying to fix).
- **Gaze upon the elephant.** Successful startups depend on multiple failure points; don't obsess over the interesting one (e.g. teachers' needs) and ignore the one that could kill you (e.g. schools can't pay).
- **Product risk vs market risk.** *Market risk*: do they want it, will they pay, are there enough of them. *Product risk*: can I build it, grow it, will they keep using it. "If you could get me X, I'd pay" shifts the burden to your product. With heavy product risk (games, a farm-fertility monitor you can't yet build), conversations prove less — you must build earlier and with less certainty.
- **Prepare your list of 3** most important things to learn per type of person (Ch. 3, Ch. 8). They will change; pick the murkiest now. Pre-planning makes it easier to face scary questions and to use serendipitous encounters.

## 7. Rules of thumb (short index)

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Opinions | Opinions are worthless. |
| Future | Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie. |
| Truth | People lie if they think it's what you want to hear. |
| Features | People know their problems, not how to solve them. |
| Goals | You're shooting blind until you understand their goals. |
| Importance | Some problems don't actually matter. |
| Watching | Watching someone do a task shows where the real inefficiencies are. |
| Searching | If they haven't looked for ways to solve it, they won't look for (or buy) yours. |
| Price | They rarely tell you what they'll pay, but often show what it's worth to them. |
| Help | People want to help but rarely do unless you give them an excuse. |
| Compliments | Compliments are fool's gold: shiny, distracting, worthless. |
| Ideas | Understand ideas and feature requests; don't obey them. |
| Ego | If you've mentioned your idea, people will try to protect your feelings. |
| Pitching | Anyone will say your idea is great if you're annoying enough about it. |
| Talking | The more you're talking, the worse you're doing. |
| Fear | Be terrified of at least one question per conversation. |
| Lukewarm | More reliable information in a "meh" than in a "wow". |
| Zoom | Start broad; don't zoom in until you've found a strong signal. |
| Process | If you don't know what you're trying to learn, don't have the conversation. |
