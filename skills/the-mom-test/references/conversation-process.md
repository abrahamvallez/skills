# Conversation process — preparing, finding people, framing, running, noting, reviewing

Source: *The Mom Test*, Ch. 4–8 and cheatsheet. Paraphrased. **[skill heuristic]** marks additions.

## 1. Keeping it casual (Ch. 4)

- Learning about a customer and their problems works better as a **quick casual chat** than as a long formal meeting. Formal first meetings are costly (a 1-hour meeting is ~4 hours with logistics), hard to get when you lack credibility, and signal "interview".
- The problem/solution/sales separation is critical to avoid bias, but the first (problem) conversation **doesn't need to be a meeting**. At events, cafés, conferences: just go to your most important question ("how did you end up getting this gig?").
- **Meeting anti-pattern**: relegating every customer conversation to a calendar block. It also makes you overlook serendipitous chances.
- If it feels like they're doing you a favour by talking to you, it's probably too formal. No need to make excuses or mention you're starting a business.
- Length: problem existence/importance ≈ 5 minutes; how they solve it ≈ quick; workflow stories 10–15 minutes (can go on if valuable); industry details can take an hour+. Product/sales meetings: carve 30+ minutes (≈ 5 lost to tardiness, 5 hello, 5 questions about goals/problems/budget, 10 show/describe the product, 5 next steps/advancement).
- Even inside a formal meeting you can keep it casual. Give as little information as possible about your idea while still nudging the discussion in a useful direction.
- Start in person where possible. Phone/Skype calls feel like scripted interviews, lose body language, and don't create the friendships that lead to warm intros — the book's opinion; some practitioners get good results by phone, so use whatever serves the purpose, but don't use calls/surveys merely to dodge awkwardness.

## 2. Commitment and advancement (Ch. 5)

- After the facts are learned, zoom in, reveal the idea and ask for **commitment** (time, reputation, money) and **advancement** (next real step of the funnel). Both outcomes — they move forward or reject — teach you.
- Without asking you get **zombie leads**: friendly people who never buy ("friend-zoned").
- Currencies and phrase verdicts: see `evaluation.md` §4–§5.
- **Fix a bad meeting**: push for a commitment at the end while being brushed off with a compliment — without being annoying or strong-arming. If they aren't excited, good: you got the information. *It's not a real lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you.*
- **Don't pitch blind**: begin with open-ended learning questions even in sales meetings ("We do X, want to buy?" → "No thanks" gives only binary feedback). Ask learning questions that pass the Mom Test, then confirm by selling — over the life of the company and within a single meeting.
- **Early evangelists / "crazy customers"**: they have the problem, know it, have budget, and have cobbled together a solution. They're emotional ("this is the worst part of my life and I'll pay now to fix it"). Not-emotional people are unlikely to be your first customers — keep them on the list but don't count on them. Keep emotional ones close.
- In early-stage sales, the real goal is **learning; revenue is a side-effect.**

## 3. Customer segmentation (Ch. 7)

- Starting too generic waters everything down (generic message, feature creep). Winners started narrow (Google: PhD students; eBay: Pez collectors) and broadened later.
- Broad segment → three problems: overwhelmed by options, can't prove yourself wrong, mixed feedback you can't interpret. "Students" or "sales organisations" hide wildly different sub-segments: you're having *one conversation each with 20 types of customer*.
- **Customer slicing**: take a segment and keep slicing. Ask: within this group, who would want it most? Would everyone buy or only some? Why do they want it (problem/goal)? Does everyone share that motivation? What other motivations exist? Which other types of people have them? Then behaviours: what are they already doing to achieve the goal or survive the problem? Where can we find the demographic groups? Where can we find people doing the workaround behaviours?
- Choose who to start with: **profitable, easy to reach, rewarding for you to build a business around.** A good segment is a **who–where pair**: if you don't know where to find them, keep slicing.
- Wrong people: (1) too broad, (2) multiple segments and you missed some (e.g. multi-sided marketplaces, kids + parents, schools: teachers/students/admin/PTA/taxpayers; key partners), (3) B2B with a complicated buying process and overlooked stakeholders. Don't just talk to the most senior/impressive people — talk to people representative of your customers.
- Number of conversations: keep going until you stop hearing new things — as few as 3–5 for a simple industry/focused segment. Over ~10 with results all over the map ⇒ segment too fuzzy. You should answer almost any customer question and move on within a week.

## 4. Finding conversations (Ch. 6)

Goal of cold conversations: to stop having them (they turn into warm intros).

- **Going to them**: cold calls/emails/LinkedIn (rejection rate is irrelevant, you need a few "yes"); seize serendipity (parties, conferences, cafés — "interview" is only weird if you think of it as an interview); **find a good excuse** (genuine topic you both care about; PhD research angle); **immerse yourself where they are** (conference circuit, free talks, favours to organisers); **landing pages** (value is the conversations you start by emailing every sign-up, not the conversion metrics); generic launch and reaching out to who likes it most.
- **Bringing them to you**: organise meetups ("HR professionals happy hour") — instant credibility; speak & teach (workshops, videos, blogging, office hours); industry blogging; get clever (e.g. host a knowledge-exchange call between respected institutions).
- **Warm intros**: 7-degrees-of-Bacon (ask everyone, even in a coworking space); industry advisors (small equity, monthly meetings, steady stream of intros); universities/professors; investors; **cash in favours** from earlier "keep me in the loop" people (sparingly — it can burn bridges).
- Keep an eye on the **advisory flip** (Ch. 6): don't go looking for customers (needy vibe, loses power); go looking for **industry and customer advisors**. You're evaluating whether *they* are a good advisor. It changes your internal narrative and keeps you in control.
- **Hack it** (Ch. 8): sometimes skip the process and just pick up the phone and ask.

## 5. Framing the meeting — Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask (Ch. 6)

If you can't avoid a proper meeting, frame it, otherwise it becomes a sales meeting by default (closed-up on pricing, attention on you, and you're not ready). Avoid "Can I interview you?", "Can I get your opinion on what we're doing?" (needy, signals compliment-seeking), or the bare "Do you have time for a coffee?" (suggests you'll waste their time).

Mnemonic: **"Very Few Wizards Properly Ask [for help]"**

1. **Vision** — the horrible problem you're fixing / the better future you're after. Don't mention your idea.
2. **Framing** — what stage you're at; that you have nothing to sell (if true).
3. **Weakness** — the specific thing you need help understanding; shows you're not a time waster.
4. **Pedestal** — why *they*, in particular, can help.
5. **Ask** — ask for help (a chat, coffee).

Parts can be merged or reordered (move the weakness earlier if it sounds pitchy and risks being deleted as spam). In the meeting, repeat the framing, name the introducer as authority, then immediately grab the reins with your first good question. **You set the agenda, keep it on topic, and propose next steps** — politely.

Template **[skill heuristic, structure from the book]**:
> Hi <name>, I'm working on <vision>. We're at an early stage and have nothing to sell — we just want to make sure we build something that actually helps. I'm struggling to understand <specific weakness>, and you've <their relevant experience>, so you could really help me cut through the fog. Do you have time in the next couple of weeks for a coffee?

## 6. Preparing (Ch. 8 "Prepping", Ch. 3 "list of 3")

Before a batch of conversations:
- Choose a focused, findable segment.
- With your team, decide the **Big 3 learning goals** (per person type). They will change; choose the murkiest now.
- If relevant, decide ideal next steps/commitments to push for.
- If conversations are the right tool, decide who to talk to.
- Write down **best guesses** about what the person cares about and wants (up to an hour) — a skeleton to update; you'll probably be wrong.
- **Desk research first** for anything the internet can answer; spend conversations on what it can't. Do basic due diligence on the person/company (≈5 minutes on LinkedIn/website).
- Prep with the whole team (business and product). Hidden-risk questions: *"If this company fails, why would it have happened?"*, *"What would have to be true for this to be a huge success?"* A gut reaction is enough.
- If you're tight on time, the only prep question you need: **"What do we want to learn from these guys?"** If you don't know, don't have the conversation.
- Spend about an hour prepping, not a week.

## 7. During the conversation (checklist)

Frame the conversation → keep it casual → ask good questions that pass the Mom Test → deflect compliments, anchor fluff, dig beneath signals → take good notes → if relevant, press for commitment and next steps. End with "who else should I talk to?" and "anything I should have asked?".

## 8. Who attends and how (Ch. 8)

- Everyone making big decisions (including tech) should attend at least some conversations; you'll learn from first-hand reactions and catch each other's mistakes.
- Best with **two people**: one talks, one takes notes and can jump in when the lead asks bad questions or misses a signal. Don't send more than two unless group-on-group.
- Solo is fine once you can take concise notes (harder to catch yourself). If shy, bring a friend as note-taker at first.
- **You can't outsource customer learning** until you have a repeatable sales/marketing process: founders must be in the room. You may hire help to set up/lead, as long as you're there listening.
- **Learning bottleneck**: when learning lives in one head, that person becomes a de-facto dictator ("the customer said so"). Share learning with the whole team promptly and faithfully.

## 9. Taking notes (Ch. 8)

- Write down **exact quotes** wrapped in quotation marks when possible; otherwise the big idea. Notes make it harder to lie to yourself and let you revisit decisions months later without redoing interviews.
- Use symbols/tags. The book uses 12; this skill suggests text tags **[skill heuristic]**:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `:)` `:(` `:|` | Excited · Angry · Embarrassed (strong emotion — dig) |
| `PAIN` | Pain or problem |
| `GOAL` | Goal / job-to-be-done |
| `OBSTACLE` | Something preventing them from solving it even though they want to |
| `WORKAROUND` | What they do instead |
| `CONTEXT` | Background |
| `FEATURE/CRITERIA` | Feature request or purchasing criteria |
| `MONEY` | Money, budgets, purchasing process |
| `NAME` | A specific person/company mentioned (ask for an intro; research competitors later) |
| `★ FOLLOW-UP` | Task to follow up on, especially next steps you promised |

- Where: notes must be sortable/mixable, combinable with teammates' notes, permanent/retrievable, and separate from to-do noise. Options: shared spreadsheet (signals in their own columns) or notes app; index cards/post-its (one quote or learning per card + signal, add date and person afterwards; sort later — pull all `PAIN` cards to find a new confirmed problem); notebooks only if dedicated. Audio recordings tend to go unlistened and can blow a casual cover (ask permission). If it's inappropriate to take notes during the chat, retreat to a corner right after and write.
- *Notes are useless if you don't look at them.*

## 10. After the conversations — review (Ch. 8)

With your team: review notes and key customer quotes → transfer notes to permanent storage if needed → update beliefs and plans → decide the next 3 big questions. Also discuss the **meta-level**: which questions worked, which didn't, what signals or questions did we miss. It's craft, not science — practise.

## 11. Pace and attitude

- Spend a week, maybe two, on early full-time conversations, then give them something to commit to. Keep talking to customers for the life of the company, casually, alongside building.
- Mistakes are inevitable ("I still ask dumb questions"); review with the team without beating anyone up. Most bad conversations can be fixed. *Talking to customers is a tool, not an obligation — if it won't help, skip it.*
