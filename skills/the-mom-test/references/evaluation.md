# Evaluating questions, interviews and discovery evidence

Rubrics derived from *The Mom Test*. The classification categories and symptom lists come from the book (Ch. 1–5, 8, cheatsheet); the checklist formats, tallies and verdict labels are **[skill heuristic]** to make the book's guidance auditable.

## 1. Question checklist (use per question)

Ask in order; the first "no" is the headline defect.

1. **About their life or about my idea?** (Ideas/opinions on the idea → fails rule 1.)
2. **Specific past behaviour, or generic / future / hypothetical?** (Fails rule 2.)
3. **Neutral, or leading / fishing for approval?** (Fishing for compliments, ego-exposing, "don't you hate…".)
4. **Does it invite a story and silence?** (Open-ended, lets them talk — rule 3.)
5. **Important?** Could any answer change or disprove my business? If not, it's trivial. Is it one of my Big 3?
6. **Zoomed too early?** Does it assume the problem matters before there is a signal?

Verdicts **[skill heuristic]**:
- ✅ **Passes** — about their life, specific past, neutral, invites a story.
- ⚠️ **Fixable** — OK as a springboard (e.g. "Do you ever X?") if followed by an anchor; or "dream product" without a why.
- ❌ **Fails** — about the idea, opinion/hypothetical/future, leading, or approval-seeking. Always provide a rewrite.

Also assess the **set**: balance (is any question *scary*?), coverage of the Big 3, order (broad → zoom), and missing closers ("who else", "anything I should have asked", next step).

## 2. Statement classification (for transcripts, notes, emails)

Tag each meaningful customer statement:

| Tag | Definition | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **FACT** | Concrete, specific, mostly past: what they did, with whom, with which tool, how long, how much it cost, what happened | Strong evidence |
| **COMMITMENT / ADVANCEMENT** | They give up something they value (time, reputation, money) or move to the next real step of the funnel | Strongest evidence |
| **COMPLIMENT** | "Love it", "cool", "keep me in the loop", "great idea" | Zero (warning flag if it ends a meeting) |
| **FLUFF** | Generic ("always/usually/never"), future ("would/will"), hypothetical ("might/could") | Zero as evidence; useful as an anchor |
| **IDEA** | Feature request / suggestion | Don't obey; understand motive |
| **EMOTION** | Anger, embarrassment, excitement | Dig — often the real problem |

Useful decomposition of FACTs (the book's note symbols): **Pain/problem · Goal/job-to-be-done · Obstacle · Workaround · Background/context · Feature request/purchasing criteria · Money/budget/purchasing process · Named person/company · Follow-up task.**

Tally **[skill heuristic]**: count statements per tag. A conversation dominated by COMPLIMENT/FLUFF/IDEA with no FACT/COMMITMENT is a "going through the motions" conversation regardless of how pleasant it felt.

## 3. Interviewer-error detection (symptoms from the book)

| Error | Symptoms in the transcript |
|---|---|
| Fishing for compliments | "I'm thinking of starting… do you think it'll work?", "do you like it?" |
| Exposing your ego (Pathos Problem) | "Here's the top-secret project I quit my job for… what do you think?", "be honest, I can take it" |
| Pitching | "No no, I don't think you get it…", "Yes, but it also does this!", long monologues |
| Too formal | "Thanks for agreeing to this interview…", "on a scale of 1 to 5…", "let's set up a meeting" for a simple chat |
| Premature zoom | Jumping to "what's your biggest problem with <our area>?" before establishing the area matters; ranking exercises that assume they care |
| Accepting compliments / fluff as data | Closing with "great, I'll send a beta key" after a lukewarm "I'd love to try it" |
| Missed dig | Strong emotion or feature request with no follow-up question |
| Talking over them | Interviewer text longer than customer text; interrupting to correct or to answer a concern they haven't finished |
| Not asking for commitment | Product/sales meeting ends without next steps or a compliment is accepted as the close |
| Skipped the scary question | No question about money, budget holder, legal/IT blockers, or the thing most likely to kill the business |
| Learning bottleneck | "You just worry about the product, I'll learn what we need", "because the customers told me so" |

## 4. Meeting outcome verdict (Ch. 5)

There is no such thing as a meeting that "just went well": **it either succeeded or failed.**

- **Succeeded**: ended with a commitment to advance to the next step — **or** a clear rejection (a strong negative data point is a good result for learning).
- **Failed**: ended with a compliment, a stalling tactic ("let me know when it launches", "keep me in the loop"), or unclear next steps; or the interviewer never asked (the real failure — rejection is not failure, not asking is).
- Quick tests: *If you don't know what happens next after a product/sales meeting, the meeting was pointless.* *The more they're giving up, the more seriously you can take their kind words.*

Good/bad closing phrases (Ch. 5):

| Phrase heard | Verdict | Why / what to do |
|---|---|---|
| "That's so cool, I love it!" | ❌ Bad | Pure compliment, zero data. Deflect and get back to business. |
| "Looks great. Let me know when it launches." | ❌ Bad | Compliment + stall. Ask for a commitment today (intro to boss/budget holder, be an alpha user, case study). |
| "I can introduce you to a couple of people when you're ready." | ❌ Bad | Generic promise. Make it concrete: who, what does "ready" mean, why not now? |
| "What are the next steps?" | ✅ Good | Advancement — but you must know your next steps. |
| "I would definitely buy that." | ❌ Bad | Highest false-positive danger. Shift to concrete current commitment: letter of intent, pre-order, deposit, intros. |
| "When can we start the trial?" | ⚠️ Maybe good | A step forward, but a "free" trial can cost them nothing. Raise the cost: case study after two weeks, team trial, card on file with no charge if cancelled. |
| "Can I buy the prototype?" | ✅ Great | The best close. |
| "When can you come back to talk to the rest of the team?" | ✅ Good | In B2B you must reach multiple people; refusal to introduce you means a dead end. |

## 5. Commitment currencies (Ch. 5)

- **Time**: clear next meeting with known goals; sitting down to give feedback on wireframes; using a trial for a non-trivial period.
- **Reputation risk**: intros to peers/team; intro to a decision maker (boss, spouse, lawyer); public testimonial or case study.
- **Cash**: letter of intent (non-binding), pre-order, deposit.
- Strong commitments combine currencies (e.g. a paid team trial). A compliment costs nothing, so it carries no data.

## 6. Evidence audit — "is this idea / discovery work solid?" **[skill heuristic built on the book]**

For each piece of evidence the user cites ("everybody loved it", "20 interviews", "survey says 80% would use it"):

1. Classify it (FACT / COMMITMENT / COMPLIMENT / FLUFF / IDEA / EMOTION).
2. Grade strength: **Strong** = concrete facts about current behaviour/spend + a commitment; **Medium** = concrete facts, no commitment; **None** = compliments, fluff, ideas, hypotheticals, surveys about future use.
3. Check segment consistency: mixed, "all over the map" feedback after ~10 conversations suggests the segment is too broad — often it is one conversation each with twenty different types of customer, not twenty conversations with one (Ch. 7).
4. Check the failure points: market risk vs product risk; who pays; who decides; the elephant in the room.
5. Look for early-evangelist signals: they have the problem, know they have it, have a budget, have already cobbled together a workaround (Ch. 5). Emotional, not just "yeah, that's a problem".
6. Verdict, honestly worded: *Validated on X (with evidence), not validated on Y, unknown on Z.* Never call something "validated" on compliments or hypotheticals. Customers who stay friendly but never buy are a dangerous mixed signal.
7. Next best conversations/commitments to close the gaps.

**Limits to state when relevant:** with heavy product risk, conversations can't prove the business (Ch. 3); surveys, phone calls and landing-page metrics are weaker than in-person conversation per the author (Ch. 6) — landing pages are best as a source of leads to talk to; early conversations don't replace building the product.

## 7. Signs of "going through the motions" (use as a checklist)

You're talking more than they are · they're complimenting you or your idea · you told them about your idea and don't know what happens next · you don't have notes · you haven't reviewed notes with your team · you got an unexpected answer and it didn't change your idea · you weren't scared of any question · you aren't sure which big question you're answering · you aren't sure why you're having the meeting.

Signs you aren't pushing for commitment and advancement: a pipeline of zombie leads · product meetings that end with a compliment · no clear next steps · meetings that "went well" · they haven't given up anything of value.

## 8. Report template (interview / communication evaluation)

```
# Evaluation — <interview / email / script> — <date>
**Verdict:** Succeeded / Failed / Mixed — <one-line reason>
**Scorecard [heuristic]:** Facts n · Commitments n · Compliments n · Fluff n · Ideas n · Missed digs n
## What we actually learned (facts only)
## Bad data collected (treat as zero)  — quote → tag → why
## Commitment / advancement obtained — or missing (what to ask next time)
## Interviewer mistakes — quote → rule broken → better line
## Missed digs & anchors — quote → follow-up to ask
## Signals about the person/segment — customer / complainer / not a customer / early-evangelist candidate
## Updates — beliefs to revise · next Big 3 · next conversation · commitment to request
```
